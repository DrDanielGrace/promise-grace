Seventeen of these, each doing exactly one thing. If you teach chemistry and any of these are useful, take them. You do not need to ask me.

The notebook Contents

Each one has three depths. Picture is what happens with no numbers. Mechanism is why it happens with the quantities named. Maths is the governing equation with the numbers live. Pick whichever suits the class.

THE NOTEBOOK

On the main page

  1. Nucleation, and why most crystals never happen

    Clusters flicker into existence and dissolve at random until one happens to cross the critical radius and runs away. The energy barrier is drawn above it. Push the supersaturation up and watch the survival rate climb.

    CLASSICAL NUCLEATION THEORY · STOCHASTIC · HANDS OFF TO THE NEXT ONE

  2. Crystal growth, with a gravity slider

    Four linked views: the crystal, the concentration field around it, the flow, and the growth rate over time. Turn gravity down and watch a depleted shell build up and growth become self limiting. The Peclet number crosses one somewhere near 0.01 g and you can find it yourself.

    DIFFUSION AND CONVECTION · PECLET NUMBER · TAKES SEEDS FROM NUCLEATION

  3. Powder diffraction, and reading a pattern

    The full pattern for a chosen lattice, peaks at their correct angles with the responsible planes labelled, and the systematic absences that are how a structure is actually identified. Then it turns around and asks you which lattice produced a pattern.

    BRAGG · SCHERRER · WILLIAMSON-HALL · RECEIVES A CRYSTAL FROM THE GROWTH SIM

  4. The phase diagram, as a set of instructions

    Drag a point around a binary phase diagram and read off what is actually in the beaker at that temperature and composition.

    LEVER RULE · BINARY EUTECTIC

  5. Solidification, and what a phase diagram is for

    Choose a composition, press cool, and watch the microstructure form beside the diagram. Dendrites at one composition, eutectic lamellae at another. This is the one that connects a phase diagram to something you can hold.

    DIRECTIONAL SOLIDIFICATION · MICROSTRUCTURE

  6. Titration, drop by drop

    The curve draws itself as the drops fall. Switch to a weak acid and the buffer region appears. Choose the wrong indicator and find out for yourself why the choice matters, because the reading comes out ninety one percent early.

    STRONG AND WEAK ACID · BUFFER REGION · INDICATOR ERROR

  7. The stalactite, with and without gravity

    A drop forms, stretches and falls, and a spike builds downward. Beside it, the same thing with almost no gravity, where the drop never falls. Marked speculative, because it is.

    SPECULATIVE · THE OPEN QUESTION

  8. Thin film interference, and why a bubble has colours

    Drag the thickness and watch the reflected colour sweep the spectrum. Put glass behind it instead of air and it becomes an anti reflection coating, and you can find the quarter wave condition by hand.

    EXACT TWO BEAM AIRY FORMULA · DARK INSTRUMENT PANEL

  9. A metal organic framework you can turn over

    Metal nodes, organic linkers, and the pore, because the hole is the point. Swap the linker for a longer one and watch the pore grow and the surface area with it. A gram of this stuff has the surface area of a football pitch.

    POROSITY · LINKER LENGTH · SURFACE AREA

  10. Thermoelectrics, and the argument the field is having

    Heat one end, watch carriers drift, watch a voltage appear. Then meet the problem: you want electrical conductivity high and thermal conductivity low, and in most materials they move together.

    SEEBECK · FIGURE OF MERIT ZT

  11. The study, walked through

    My undergraduate research project taken apart step by step, including the parts I would do differently now.

    SURVEY DESIGN · WHAT I GOT WRONG

WHAT I AM LEARNING

On the mission planner

  1. Why the pH scale is logarithmic

    The same number on a linear axis and a log axis, side by side. The linear bar disappears, which is the entire reason the log scale exists.

    LOGARITHMS · FOURTEEN POWERS OF TEN

  2. Arrhenius, plotted twice

    Rate against temperature is a curve you cannot read off. The log of the rate against one over temperature is a straight line whose slope hands you the activation energy. Same data, both ways, at once.

    ACTIVATION ENERGY · LINEARISATION

  3. Band gaps, and which light gets through

    Drag the wavelength. A photon only lifts an electron across if it carries enough energy, and below that the material is simply transparent to it.

    PHOTON ENERGY · SIX REAL ABSORBERS

  4. Where the sunlight actually goes

    The real measured solar spectrum with a band gap cutting it in two. One shaded area is light too weak to be absorbed at all, the other is energy thrown away as heat the instant it is. For silicon that is half the sunlight before anything electrical happens.

    ASTM G173 AM1.5G · INTEGRATED, NOT SKETCHED · HANDS OFF TO THE NEXT ONE

  5. The Shockley-Queisser limit, and a second junction

    Detailed balance computed on that spectrum, giving 33.7 percent at 1.34 eV. The whole energy budget is stacked so you can see the two loss areas from the previous simulation as the outer bands. Then add a second junction underneath and watch the ceiling climb to 45.7 percent.

    DETAILED BALANCE · CURRENT MATCHED TANDEM · RECEIVES A GAP

  6. Bragg's law, one reflection at a time

    Two X rays off neighbouring planes. Move the angle and the spacing and watch the extra path length become a whole number of wavelengths, which is the moment the detector sees a peak.

    n LAMBDA = 2d SIN THETA

IF YOU TEACH

Using these with a class

Every one of these works from a link, so you can send a class straight to a single one without them wading through my notebook first. The settings are in the address bar, which means if you set one up the way you want it, the link you copy carries that exact configuration.

They all have three depths. If your class has not met the maths yet, put it on Picture and the numbers disappear without the physics changing. Mechanism names the quantities. Maths shows the governing equation with the values live.

Every simulation also has a show the code control that reveals the function actually running it, and underneath that, a list of what it assumes and where it stops being true. I put that there because I got several of these wrong the first time and had to find out the hard way which corners I had cut. A simulation that will not tell you what it is faking is not teaching anybody anything.
