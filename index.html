How does reduced gravity change the way materials form?

I am a chemistry graduate from the University of Ilorin. What I keep coming back to is what happens to crystal growth and solidification when you take gravity out of the equation. This is my notebook. It has my research, the things I have taught, the study I ran, and a few questions I have not worked out yet.

ENTRY 01

The question

the thing I keep coming back to

On Earth, gravity pulls a growing crystal down through its own solution. Warm fluid rises, cool fluid sinks, and the crystal grows inside an environment that will not sit still. You end up with smaller crystals carrying more defects.

Take gravity away and convection largely stops. Growth becomes diffusion limited. Crystals come out larger, cleaner, more ordered.

That difference is not a curiosity. It changes what we can learn about how materials assemble themselves, and it changes what we can build.

ENTRY 02

Phase diagrams, properly explained

this took me a while to really get

A phase diagram is a map. Temperature up the side, composition along the bottom. Every point on it answers one question: if I hold this mixture at this temperature until it settles, what will I actually have?

Drag the point around. The panel beside it shows what you would be holding.

Reading one in plain words

Start at the top. Everything up there is liquid, because it is too hot for anything to hold together as a solid.

Come down and you hit a line called the liquidus. That is the temperature where the first solid appears. Not where it all freezes. Where it starts.

Keep going and you hit the solidus. That is where the last of the liquid finally goes. Below it, everything is solid.

The gap between those two lines is the part people find strange. In that band you are holding liquid and solid together, at the same temperature, at the same time. Nothing is wrong. That is what the mixture does.

Here is where students go wrong. They read the composition axis and think it tells them what the liquid is made of. It does not. It tells you the composition of the whole mixture you started with. Inside the two phase region the liquid and the solid each have their own composition, and neither of them matches the number you started at. Read those off the ends of the tie line, not off the label.

The lever rule is the part that looks like maths and is really just a seesaw. Draw the horizontal tie line across the two phase region. Your overall composition sits somewhere along it. The further it sits from one end, the more you have of the phase at the other end. Sit in the middle and you have half and half.

The second mistake, and it follows from the first: people measure to the near end and report that phase. It is the opposite. Long arm, large amount, and the arm you measure points away from the phase you are counting.

A eutectic is the composition where the liquidus reaches its lowest point. That mixture melts at a lower temperature than either pure component on its own, which is the whole reason solder works. It also freezes all at once, with no mushy band in between, because at that one composition the liquidus and solidus meet.

ENTRY 03

A titration you can get wrong

every student overshoots the first time

25.00 cm³ of hydrochloric acid in the flask, sodium hydroxide in the burette, both 0.100 M. A few drops of phenolphthalein. The rough run is already done, so 24.50 cm³ is in and this is the careful one.

Add it a drop at a time and watch the flask rather than the burette.

There it is. This is the mistake I want students to make early, in a practice run, with cheap reagents. You do not forget the endpoint after you have blown past it once. Half of teaching practical chemistry is engineering safe mistakes.

ENTRY 04

The study I ran

1,000 students, and a few things I would do differently

  1. The question

    Were students actually using the internet for academic work, or were they telling themselves they were?

  2. The method

    If it were yours to design, which would you pick?

    Interviews would have told me why, in their own words, and I would have learned things I did not know to ask about. But thirty interviews is a good week of work, and thirty students cannot tell you what a faculty does.

    An observational study would have shown me what they did rather than what they said they did, which is the honest version. It also needs access to their devices or their network, and I had neither.

    A survey is what I chose. It was the only one that reached enough students to say something about the population, and I could run it alongside a full teaching load. I knew going in that I was collecting what people reported about themselves, and that is the trade I made.

  3. The sample

    1,000 undergraduate and postgraduate students across chemistry education and other STEM fields. I went through department contacts and class groups rather than posting it publicly, because I wanted to know which faculty each response came from.

  4. The analysis

    SPSS. Descriptives first to see the shape of it, then hypothesis testing to check whether the differences between groups were real or just noise in the sample.

    Hypothesis testing in plain terms: you assume there is no difference, then you work out how surprising your data would be if that were true. Surprising enough, and you stop believing the assumption.

  5. What I would change

    The self reporting is the weak point and I knew it at the time. I asked students how they used the internet and I recorded their answers as though they were behaviour. They are not. They are what people believe about themselves, filtered through what they think a researcher wants to hear.

    I would fix it by pairing the survey with something I could observe. A smaller group keeping a log for two weeks, or reported hours checked against something measurable.

    The other thing is the sample. Going through department contacts gave me clean faculty data and it also meant I mostly reached students who turn up and are on the class lists. The ones who do not are exactly the ones whose habits I most wanted to see. I would build a route to them next time, even a small one.

    I did the research methods course at the University of London after all of this. Reading it back now, I can see the study I should have run.

ENTRY 05

The stalactite question

I wrote this years before I knew it mattered

I wrote this while preparing a lesson on water hardness. I was explaining to students how caves build themselves. Water drips, leaves a little calcium carbonate behind, and over thousands of years that becomes rock.

I did not think of it as anything except a nice thing to tell a class. Then I started studying crystal growth properly and realised I had been describing it the whole time. A stalactite is a crystal growing very slowly out of solution, one layer at a time.

Here is what I cannot stop thinking about. A stalactite exists because gravity makes the water drip. The drip is the whole mechanism. Take gravity away and the water does not fall at all. It holds onto the ceiling as a bead, held by surface tension, and it stays there.

So what forms instead? Does the mineral come out of solution evenly all around the bead instead of building downward? Would you get a shell rather than a spike? Nothing sinks, so nothing settles unevenly. Does it come out cleaner?

I do not know. I am fairly sure someone has looked at this, and I have not found the paper yet. But it is the same question as the one I want to research, just written at cave scale instead of laboratory scale. Gravity is not a background condition in crystal growth. It is doing work. I want to find out how much.

If you know what actually happens here, or you know the paper I have not found, I would really like you to tell me.

ENTRY 06

Explaining things

two years of finding out which explanations actually work

Every one of these is lifted from my notes. The pattern is always the same. Say the thing, then say the thing people get wrong, because the second one is what actually sticks.

Why water is not written in the equation

When you soften hard water, water is everywhere in the reaction and it never appears in the equation. That looks like an error the first time you see it.

It is not. We only write substances that are chemically reacting. Water is the medium they are reacting in.

No new water is formed. All the substances are already dissolved in water before anything happens. Water is the solvent, not a reactant, so it stays out of the equation.

Soft water is not the same as safe water

Permutit softens water by taking out the calcium and magnesium and putting sodium in their place. Think of it as a swap, not a clean.

Removing hardness does not mean the water is safe or pure to drink. It does not remove all salts, it only replaces some of them. Bacteria are untouched. Soft and safe are two different questions.

Why distilled water is not ideal for drinking

Boil water, catch the steam, cool it back down and you have distilled water. 100 percent pure. No salts, no germs.

So yes, it is drinkable. It is just not good for you day to day, because it has no minerals at all. And most bottled and sachet water is treated water, not distilled water, which people mix up constantly.

Why soft water corrodes lead pipes and hard water does not

Hard water leaves a mineral coating inside the pipe. That layer of scale sits between the water and the lead, so the water never touches the metal directly.

Soft water lays down no coating. Nothing separates the water from the lead, so lead leaches straight into it. The softer water is the more dangerous one here, which is the opposite of what students expect.

What lead does once it is in you is why I teach this properly: hypertension, stroke, kidney damage, and lowered IQ in children.

How caves build themselves

Stalactites grow down from the roof. Stalagmites grow up from the ground. Water drips through limestone, leaves a tiny bit of calcium carbonate behind each time, and over many years that becomes rock.

They are built bit by bit, not suddenly. It is like adding cement layer by layer. They grow very slowly.

This one turned out to matter more than I thought. It is Entry 05.

What makes benzene different

Benzene is the mother of the aromatic compounds. Six carbons in a ring with alternating double bonds, C6H6, and it counts as unsaturated because of those double bonds.

Unsaturated normally means it will happily add things across the double bond. Benzene does not. Its main reaction is substitution, where a hydrogen gets swapped out for another group and the ring survives intact. The ring is stable and it does not want to give that up.

Why amino acids change with pH

An amino acid carries an amino group and a carboxyl group at the same time, so it can act as an acid or a base depending on what is around it.

In the stomach, around pH 2, the amino group picks up a hydrogen and the molecule carries a positive charge. In blood, around pH 7, it is a zwitterion, positive at one end and negative at the other, so it balances out. In the small intestine the carboxyl group loses its hydrogen and the whole thing goes negative.

The charge is not a side effect. It is what keeps the molecule dissolved and it is what prepares it to be absorbed. A neutral molecule would not travel in blood the way this does.

ENTRY 07

Things I built for students

be creative with it

Carbon and its compounds

A mini 3D scene built on a cardboard box base. Trees and plants, humans and animals drawn or moulded in clay, a factory or a burning candle, and arrows through the atmosphere showing carbon dioxide moving. Labelled photosynthesis, respiration, combustion.

The pH scale

A vertical colour strip board. Red for strong acid, orange for weak acid, green for neutral, blue for weak base, purple for strong base. Students paste their own tested samples beside the colours and add small indicator result circles.

Acids in the environment

A split cardboard diorama. The left side is a normal environment with green trees and clean water. The right side shows acid rain damage, yellowed leaves and corroded metal. Cotton wool for clouds, with drawn raindrops labelled acid rain.

Water and purification

A three stage flow board. Dirty water, then a filtration system, then clean water. Cut out arrows showing movement, and a small filter column built or drawn from cotton, sand and charcoal. Labelled sedimentation, filtration, decantation.

Hydrocarbons and crude oil

A collage style display. Transport with petrol and diesel, cooking gas, plastic products, asphalt roads. Students paste real product wrappers and connect each one back to crude oil.

Creativity · Accuracy · Presentation · Understanding

I set these instead of essays because I wanted them to make a thing with their hands. The student who builds the filter column out of cotton and sand and charcoal never has to be told the difference between filtration and decantation again. They watched it happen. An essay on the same topic comes back to me correct and gone by Friday.

ENTRY 08

At the bench

a school lab on a small budget teaches you a lot

I prepare my own reagents, run titrimetric analysis and qualitative inorganic analysis with the classes, and keep the lab safe and working on very little money. When the equipment cannot do the job, I use PhET simulations rather than skip the practical, because a student who has only ever read about a reaction has not really met it.

Working with a small budget makes you decide what a practical is actually for. Usually it is one idea, and you can nearly always get to that idea with what is already in the cupboard.

ENTRY 09

The long way round

worth saying plainly

My first two years at university were not good. I was learning how to study at the same time as I was learning chemistry, and it showed. It got better every year after that. My final year was my strongest, and the courses I did best in were the hardest ones: coordination chemistry, reaction kinetics, radio nuclear chemistry, organometallics. My research project came out at the top of the range.

I am not going to pretend the early years did not happen. But I would ask anyone reading this to look at the direction rather than the average.
