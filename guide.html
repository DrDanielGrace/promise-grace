THE WALKTHROUGH

One real question, worked all the way through

Here is the question I actually want to spend the next several years on: why does a crystal grown in space come out cleaner than one grown here?

It is not a rhetorical question and I am not about to tell you the answer in a paragraph. Three simulations answer it between them, in order, and each one hands its result to the next. That is also how the whole site is built, so by the end of this you will know how to use it.

Step one. A crystal has to survive being born.

Before anything can grow, a handful of molecules has to happen to clump together and then happen to stay clumped. Almost none of them do. Making a surface costs energy and that cost grows with the square of the radius. Being solid rather than dissolved pays energy back and that payoff grows with the cube. Cubes beat squares eventually, so there is a size where the cost stops winning, and a cluster that reaches it runs away and becomes a crystal.

Below that size it is more likely to fall apart. Watch it happen. Clusters appear, wobble, and mostly vanish. Then push the supersaturation slider up and watch how many more get through.

The number that survived is not decoration. It is about to matter, because every survivor becomes a crystal competing for the same solute.

Step two. Now turn gravity down.

This is the one to actually play with. Drag gravity from 1 g towards zero and watch the shaded ring around the crystal, because that ring is the whole answer.

Under gravity, warm depleted solution rises away from the crystal and fresh solution falls in to replace it. Convection never stops feeding it. So the crystal grows fast, and it grows unevenly, because whichever face happens to be facing the flow gets more.

Take gravity away and nothing stirs. The crystal eats the solute closest to it, and a depleted shell builds up around it. Now the only way anything reaches the surface is diffusion, which is slow and comes from every direction equally. Growth slows down. And because it slows down, it becomes even.

That is the part nobody says out loud: microgravity does not make crystals better by being gentler. It makes them better by making growth self limiting.

The Péclet number in that readout is the ratio of the two kinds of transport, flow against diffusion. Find where it crosses one. That is the threshold, somewhere near 0.01 g, and you can locate it yourself rather than take my word for it.

When the crystal finishes growing it hands itself to the next simulation. That is a real handoff and the page says so at both ends.

Step three. Prove the difference is measurable.

Uniform is a word. If I want to claim these two crystals are different, I have to show a measurement that can tell them apart, and the measurement is X ray diffraction.

Fire X rays at a crystal and they only bounce back at particular angles, the ones where the reflections from neighbouring planes of atoms arrive in step. Where those angles are tells you the spacing. Which reflections are missing tells you the lattice. And how wide the peaks are tells you how big and how ordered the crystallite is, because a small or strained crystal smears its peaks out.

Send the 1 g crystal and the peaks come out broad. Send the near zero g one and they come out sharp. That relationship is the Scherrer equation and it is real.

So: a crystal has to survive a size barrier before it can grow at all; gravity keeps stirring fresh solution in and that is what makes growth fast and uneven; and the difference that makes is not aesthetic, it is measurable in a diffractometer.

That is my research question, and you now know how the site works, and I did not have to teach you either of those separately.

If you want the honest caveat: the peak widths in that last one are computed, but the strain figure travelling between the two simulations is a declared proxy, not a measurement, and it says so in three separate places. I would rather label it than let it quietly become a fact.

THE THREE PENS

How to read my handwriting

There are scans of my actual notes all over this site, and they are the most credible thing on it. They also use a system I have been using since university without ever explaining it to anybody, so here it is.

A crop of the water purification page. All three pens are in this one corner.

Blue carries the argument.
The actual chemistry. If it is in blue, it is the thing I am claiming.
Red marks the mistake.
Not my mistake. The mistake people make. Red is where I have written down the thing students get wrong, because after two years of teaching I know that the misconception is what sticks, not the correct version. On this site red only ever means that, and nothing else. It is not used for errors, or warnings, or anything to do with the interface.
Orange is an afterthought squeezed into the margin.
Something I thought of later and had nowhere to put. Usually a connection to something else, or a definition I realised I had assumed.

The site uses the same three colours for the same three jobs, in the same order of importance, so the argument, the correction and the afterthought are told apart the same way on the screen as they are on the paper. Every scan also has a full transcription next to it, both because my handwriting is my handwriting and because a screen reader cannot read a photograph.

THE QUESTION NOBODY ASKS

Why a chemistry teacher built this

You are probably wondering, and it would be a strange thing to ask me directly, so I will answer it here.

I trained as a chemistry teacher and I teach secondary school chemistry in Lagos. That is not a detour from this. It is the reason the explanations on this site are any good.

Here is what teaching does to you. You cannot hide behind a formula in front of thirty fifteen year olds. If you only half understand why the log is in the pH scale, they will find the gap in about ninety seconds, and they will find it by asking the one question you were hoping nobody would ask. So you go home and you actually learn it, properly, down to the part you had been skating over.

I have had to do that with a lot of mechanisms now. Rate equations. Buffer regions. Why an indicator is not the same thing as the equivalence point. Lattice structures. Every one of them I thought I understood until I had to say it out loud to somebody who did not.

So when I say a depleted shell is what makes growth in microgravity self limiting, I am not reciting that. I built the simulation, got it wrong, measured it, and fixed it, and then I had to work out how to say it in three sentences. That second part is the teaching. It turns out to be the same skill as writing a paper somebody can follow.

Truthfully, I am still learning. There is a lot on this site I would not have been able to build a year ago, and there is a question in entry 05 I still cannot answer. But if you cannot explain a mechanism clearly you do not understand it yet, and I have had to explain rather a lot of mechanisms.

ONE LAST THING

I am not going to tell you to go and explore

I am going to give you something specific instead.

There is one question on this site I cannot answer. A stalactite exists because gravity makes the water drip. The drip is the entire mechanism. Take gravity away and the water does not fall at all, it holds onto the ceiling as a bead under surface tension and stays there.

So what forms instead? Does the mineral come out all around the bead rather than building downward? Do you get a shell rather than a spike? Nothing sinks, so nothing settles unevenly. Does it come out cleaner?
